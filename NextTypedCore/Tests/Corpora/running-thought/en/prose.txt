The harbour was quiet by the time the last of the freight had been unloaded. A thin rain moved in from the west and settled over the cranes, and the men on the quay pulled their collars up without saying much about it. Somebody had left a radio playing in the weighbridge office, and the sound of it carried further than it should have across the wet concrete.

Elena had been keeping the tally sheets for three winters and she no longer needed the printed form to know where the numbers went. She wrote the container marks in the left column, the seal numbers in the middle, and whatever the driver told her about the previous stop in the margin. Most of it was never read again. Once in a while an inspector would ask for a sheet from six months back, and then the margin was the only part that mattered.

It is easy to underestimate how much of a working port is bookkeeping. The ships arrive and the cranes swing and the photographs in the trade magazines show none of the paper. But a container that cannot be matched to a manifest is a container that cannot leave the yard, and a yard that cannot clear itself fills up in about four days. Everyone who has worked a season knows this. Nobody who has not worked a season believes it.

The new system was supposed to remove the paper entirely. It removed about half of it and added a second screen, which the crane operators disliked, and a set of codes that nobody could remember without a laminated card. Two of the older men kept their notebooks anyway and reconciled them against the terminal every evening. When the network went down in February, theirs were the only records anybody could use, and the argument about the notebooks stopped for a while after that.

Rain in that part of the coast is not dramatic. It is persistent, which is worse for cardboard and better for the reservoirs, and it makes the difference between a shift that runs to schedule and a shift that runs an hour long. The forecast is consulted every morning and believed about half the time. On the wall of the canteen there is a chart of the previous year's delays, and the wet months are visible in it without anyone having to explain the axis.

Later, when the port was written about, the account concentrated on the tonnage and the two strikes and the arrival of the larger vessels. That is a fair summary of what changed. It is not a description of what the work was like, which was mostly waiting, followed by a short period in which everything had to happen at once, followed by more waiting. Elena said once that she had spent a decade being either bored or frightened and almost nothing in between, and that this was true of most jobs worth having.

The apprentices were told to learn the knots first and the paperwork second, which was the reverse of what actually mattered but had the advantage of being teachable in an afternoon. Some of them stayed. Most did not. Those who stayed usually did so because somebody older took an interest, and that interest was rarely arranged by anyone in the office. It happened on the quay, in the rain, over a tally sheet somebody had filled in wrongly.
